# Article Voice

Circulation Studio. How articles are written.
Version 1.0, 2026-08-01.

## What this covers

Articles, whitepapers, field notes, guides and FAQ answers. Everything
published under /yelp/ and /library/.

House style lives in 06_Content_Guidelines_Style.md: banned words,
capitalization, numbers, dates, terminology, client email, proposals.
That file still governs. This one covers only the register articles are
written in, and it replaces 07_Voice_Personas.md, whose three personas
do not describe how this work is actually written and one of which
instructs em dashes.

## The stance

A partner thinking out loud in front of a client who already trusts
them, working out whether Yelp fits.

Not a pitch. Not a defense of the platform. Not a critique of it. An
assessment, handed to someone who could run it themselves.

That is how the work actually happens: Yelp comes up inside an existing
relationship, and the conversation is whether it is the right fit. The
reader of an article has no relationship with the studio, and writing to
a stranger the way you write to a client who trusts you is what makes a
stranger trust you.

Two readers, one voice:

**Considered.** Dentists, solar contractors. Higher ticket, longer
decision, often a practice manager doing the research. Will read 5,000
sourced words. Pitched constantly and allergic to being handled.

**Operational.** Garage door, windshield, restaurants. Decisions made
between jobs. Wants to know what it costs, whether it works for their
trade, and what happens if it does not.

The register does not change between them. The scaffolding does. A field
note for a garage door installer carries no methodology block, and a
whitepaper does.

## Measured baseline

From the Yelp advertising whitepaper, 5,296 words, which is the register
this document describes:

- 243 sentences, median 19 words, 14% under ten. Varied, not uniformly short.
- Zero hedges. No arguably, somewhat, perhaps, in our view.
- Eleven explicit limits. Directional, caveat, best-case, not a controlled test, self-commissioned, unaudited.
- 63 parenthetical citations against 5 sentences with "we" as subject. Twelve to one.

That ratio is the discipline. The evidence carries the argument and the
studio stays out of the frame.

## Seven rules

### 1. Open on the mechanics, not the reader's feelings

The reader often arrives frustrated. Acknowledging that in the first
line is the writer narrating the conversation instead of having it.
Start with how the thing works and let the reader locate themselves.

**Yes.** Yelp Ads work for some home service businesses and waste money
for others. The difference is not luck and it is not the platform. It
comes down to four conditions, and you can check all four yourself
before spending anything.

**No.** We get asked this constantly, usually by someone who has already
been burned. Here is the honest version.

**No.** Most people asking this have already tried it. They spent a few
hundred dollars, got some calls that went nowhere, and cancelled.

Openings are declarative. A rhetorical question is available and rarely
worth it. FAQ headings are the exception, because the format is a
question.

**Guides are the one exception to this rule.** A guide opens with the
procedure, not with how the thing works. The reader has already decided
to do it and is looking for step one; explaining the mechanism first is
the writer establishing authority the reader did not ask for. Mechanics
appear in a guide only where a step is meaningless without them, and
then inside the step.

**Yes.** Open your Yelp business account and go to Photos. You need ten
usable images before any of the rest of this matters.

**No.** Yelp's ranking model weighs photo count, recency and engagement,
so before adding photos it is worth understanding how that works.

### 2. State a limit as a fact, never as a softener

Limits belong in the writing constantly. Hedges never do.

A limit is information: this figure is self-commissioned, this sample is
202 people, this range is unaudited. A hedge is the writer protecting
themselves: arguably, somewhat, in our view, results may vary.

**Yes.** The filter is not personal and it is not negotiable. It applies
the same rules to advertisers and non-advertisers, and it moves reviews
in and out over time. Plan around it rather than fighting it.

**Yes.** We cite Yelp's own 4x figure because it is the only measurement
available at that scale. It is also self-commissioned, uncontrolled, and
almost certainly optimistic.

**No.** Platform-published performance figures are marketing.

### 3. Keep the studio out of the sentence

Twelve citations for every "we". The default subject is the platform,
the number, or the reader's business.

"We" is earned when the studio's own judgment is the content: why a
source was chosen, what a figure is worth, what the studio will not
claim. That is disclosure. "We would rather say that now" is posture,
and posture is what a reader who trusts you does not need.

**Yes.** As a certified partner we get API access, partner pricing, and
a direct support channel. What that buys you is a campaign that is
actually tuned and an ad dollar that goes further. It does not buy
preferential treatment in the auction, because nothing does.

**No.** We would rather say that now than manage a campaign that was
never going to work.

**A piece may open on "we" where the studio's own judgment is the
content.** Rule 1 sends openings to the mechanics, and that holds when
the mechanics are the subject. When the subject IS the judgment, why
this source and not that one, what the studio will not claim, what a
number is worth, then the studio is the mechanism and burying it behind
a platform sentence is evasion dressed as restraint. This is the same
test as the rest of the rule, applied to the first line: disclosure
earns "we", posture does not.

**Yes.** We use Yelp's own 4x figure here, and it is the weakest number
in this document. It is self-commissioned and uncontrolled, and no
independent measurement exists at that scale.

**No.** We have spent years learning what actually moves the needle on
Yelp, and we want to share it with you.

### 4. Pair every capability with its limit

The most persuasive sentences in the whitepaper are the ones that state
what something does and immediately what it does not. Certification buys
tooling and pricing, not auction advantage. The filter is dynamic, and
advertising does not influence it.

This is not balance for its own sake. An unpaired capability claim reads
as a pitch, and the reader has heard the pitch.

### 5. Conclude

An assessment that never concludes is useless. Give the reader a rule
they can run themselves, with a threshold.

**Yes.** Run the six questions above against your own business. Five or
six yeses and Yelp belongs in your mix. Two or fewer and the money is
better spent on the foundation, or on a channel that fits your category.

**Yes.** An agency is worth it when the spend is large enough that a few
points of waste exceeds the fee. Below that, fix the three settings
yourself and keep the money.

Telling the reader not to hire you, with a number attached, is the
strongest version of this. It is also the reason the rest is believed.

### 6. Do not trade evidence for pace

Short is not the goal. Plain is the goal.

Where the numbers are the argument, print the numbers. One sentence of
synthesis afterwards, saying no more than the figures support.

**Yes.** Yelp's filings show average cost-per-click rising year over
year for five consecutive quarters: +9%, +11%, +14%, +6% across 2025,
then +8% in Q1 2026. Read alongside a 6% decline in paying locations,
the picture is a thinning advertiser pool bidding up a smaller number of
clicks.

**No.** CPCs are up. Five straight quarters, per Yelp's own filings.
Fewer advertisers, paying more.

Citation density follows format. Heavy in a whitepaper, absent in a
40-word FAQ answer.

### 7. Cut the line rather than rewrite it

The failure mode in this voice is not filler. It is overwriting: a
sentence pleased with its own construction, usually an elegant negation
of something nobody claimed.

When a line is not earning, delete it. Keep the fact.

**Was.** Added to as the work produces something worth writing down,
rather than on a schedule. Last reviewed July 2026.

**Is.** Last reviewed July 2026.

The same test kills "a cluster with one entry today is a cluster we are
still in." It sounds good, takes a second read, and exists to make an
absence sound intentional.

## Register by kind

The frontmatter `kind` value sets the scaffolding, not the voice.

| kind | Reader | Scaffolding | Citation density |
|---|---|---|---|
| whitepaper | Considered | Cover, exec summary, methodology, references | Heavy, every figure sourced inline |
| essay | Either | Prose, occasional pull quote | Light, sourced where a claim needs it |
| fieldnote | Operational | Prose, one or two blocks | Light |
| guide | Operational | Steps, before you start, watch out | Minimal, the procedure is the content |
| faq | Operational | Question and answer | Usually none. Answer in 40 to 80 words |

An FAQ answer with no statistic in it is correct. Fast, conditional,
done.

**Yes.** For most trades in metro markets, yes, provided the average job
covers a competitive click and the profile has enough reviews to convert
the traffic. In thin markets or on low-ticket work, usually not.

## Banned in articles

In addition to the house list in 06.

- Hedges: arguably, somewhat, fairly, quite, perhaps, in our view, it could be argued, results may vary
- Conversation narration: we get asked this constantly, here is the part nobody wants to hear, let us be honest, the truth is
- Posture: we would rather, we believe strongly, our philosophy is
- Elegant negation: any construction whose second half denies a thing nobody proposed
- Enthusiasm as evidence: powerful, incredible, game-changing, must-have
- Unpaired capability claims

## The test

Read the sentence and ask what the reader now knows that they did not.
If the answer is nothing, and the sentence was doing tone, cut it.
